Clipper Mug

Europa Clipper · an unofficial explainer

A mug that is a map

This is Kevin's coffee mug. Printed on it is a map of a real spacecraft: NASA's Europa Clipper, which is flying to Jupiter right now to find out whether its icy moon Europa hides an ocean.

The mug is not a model of the ship. It is a map of its sides. The bottom of the mug is the side that faces Europa. The open top is the side with the big radio dish. Going round the mug you pass one solar panel, the front, the other solar panel, and the handle.

In the 3D view is the real spacecraft, built from published sizes, flying past Europa the way it will after it reaches Jupiter in 2030. Each station picks one thing printed on the mug and shows what that thing does.

The print is titled "THE EUROPA CLIPPER GEOMETRY REFERENCE TOOL" and, under it, "STANDARD CLOSEST APPROACH (SCA) CONFIG". SCA is a real term: near 10,000 km from Europa the solar arrays rotate to a fixed position for the REASON radar, "known as the standard closest approach (SCA) configuration" [C25].

The mug is an unrolled map of the spacecraft body frame. Its vertical axis is Y (+Y nadir at the base, −Y at the opening), its circumference runs +X → +Z → −X → −Z (handle) [print]. The real body's long axis is Z (the launch-vehicle centreline) [FK], so treat the mug as a frame diagram, not a scale model.

The 3D model uses the quick-facts bus envelope (3 m × 4.7 m × 4 m), a 30.5 m array span, a 3 m HGA, an 8.5 m magnetometer boom and the FK boresights [JPL][FK][Co25]. Europa and Jupiter are not to scale (see station 01). The Europa flyby speed is unconfirmed; 4–5 km/s is supported [T24][K25].

Go deeper

Why a mug?

It is the kind of cheat-sheet an engineer who plans the spacecraft's moves would keep, printed on a mug so you can turn it in your hand the way the ship turns in space.

Body-frame mnemonics matter because nearly every pointing rule is written in that frame: "+Y to nadir", "HGA on −Y", "+Z to ram at closest approach" [FK][M23][P24]. The mug encodes those three rules in its shape.

How to use this site

Drag the ship to spin it, pinch or scroll to zoom, tap any part for its name. Dotted words have definitions. The ELI5 / Engineer switch at the top changes every explanation.

Engineer mode adds frames, numbers, units and source tags; tap a tag to open the sources list in station 12. The ☰ menu lists every station and the glossary.

01 · The frame

Which way is up

Spacecraft have no up or down, so engineers paint three arrows on the ship and name them X, Y and Z. Every instruction, like "point Y at the moon", uses those arrows.

On Europa Clipper, +Y is the side that faces Europa during a flyby (that is the mug's bottom, with the down-arrow). −Y is the big dish side (the mug's open top). +Z is the way the ship is moving when it passes closest to Europa (the big yellow face). +X and −X run out along the two solar wings.

Turn on the ghost mug and you can see the mug wrapped round the ship. One honest warning: the real ship is long along Z, like a tower, not along Y like a mug. The mug is a map of the sides, not a model.

Frame EUROPAM_SPACECRAFT (ID −159000) [FK]: the origin is "at the center of the circle of the Spacecraft to Launch Vehicle Separation Interface"; +Y "is pointed towards the direction of nadir pointed instruments"; +Z "is nominally parallel to the launch vehicle centerline"; +X "completes the right-handed frame, nominally parallel to the long axis of the solar arrays".

At closest approach +Y is nadir and +Z is velocity (the FK diagrams label "+Ysc and Nadir @CA" and "+Zsc and Velocity @CA") [FK]. The HGA and MGA point −Y [M23].

The mug maps the frame onto a cylinder with Y as its axis. The yellow rectangle is the +Z face; its top edge is the gold line the print labels "−Y EDGE OF VAULT" (the vault's −Y face; that name is unconfirmed as an official feature). The pale discs on either side of the handle are one disc centred on −Z [print]. The real stack is Propulsion Module then Avionics Module (the vault) along Z, with the nadir deck on +Y of the vault [S25][P24], so the physical long axis is Z, not Y.

Go deeper

Right-handed, and why it matters

Point your right index finger along X and your middle finger along Y; your thumb is Z. Every spacecraft uses this rule so nobody ever argues about which way "+Z" is.

X × Y = Z. The FK defines Y and Z from hardware (nadir instruments, LV centreline) and lets X "complete the right-handed frame", nominally along the arrays [FK].

What "nadir" and "ram" mean

Nadir means "straight down at the moon". Ram means "the way we are moving", the direction that hits gas and dust head-on, like bugs on a windscreen.

Before 40,000 km altitude the spacecraft "will twist around the nadir axis to put SUDA and MASPEX into the velocity direction" (the nadir-ram attitude) [C25]; +Z is then the Kepler ram direction, within 2.5° for SUDA on ≥ 90 % of planned flybys [K25].

Why the ghost mug is not to scale

If the mug were a true model, the solar wings would stretch ten mug-widths from tip to tip.

Bus 3 m × 4.7 m × 4 m; deployed span 30.5 m; HF radar antennas 17.6 m [JPL]. The ghost is scaled so its wall passes through the vault faces (Ø ≈ 6 m, height ≈ 5 m).

02 · The flyby

Closest approach

Clipper does not orbit Europa. It orbits Jupiter and swoops past Europa 49 times, dipping as low as 25 km above the ice, then racing back out of Jupiter's radiation. Each swoop is a flyby, and the lowest point is called closest approach.

A few hours before closest approach the ship turns its +Y side to face the moon and its +Z side forward. Then, about 10,000 km out, the two solar wings turn to a fixed angle (−90°) so the radar antennas on their edges look down at the ice. That pose is the "standard closest approach configuration" on the mug, SCA for short.

Scrub the timeline to watch it happen. The ground rushes past at a few kilometres every second.

The tour has 49 Europa flybys at typical closest-approach distances of 25–100 km, plus 7 Ganymede and 9 Callisto flybys [P24][JPL]. Flybys rather than a Europa orbit let the spacecraft "quickly dip into the harsh radiation environment … and, just as quickly, escape" [C25].

Sequence [C25]: just before 40,000 km altitude the spacecraft twists about the nadir axis to put SUDA and MASPEX into the velocity direction; near 10,000 km the arrays actuate to the fixed SCA position "with the edge of the arrays on which the VHF and HF antennas are mounted pointed toward nadir" (array angle −90° [C25 Fig. 17]); Sun tracking resumes during the turn to Earth-point about half a day after C/A.

During the flyby the HGA points "upward and away from the moon, and not toward Earth" [JPL], so tracking uses the fan-beam and low-gain antennas (station 08). Ground-track speed is unconfirmed; EIS is designed for up to ∼4.5 km/s at 25 km altitude and SUDA expects impacts at 4–5 km/s [T24][K25]. The altitude profile here is a schematic hyperbola, not a trajectory file.

Go deeper

Why everything looks at the same patch

Pointing the cameras down and the "sniffers" forward at the same time means every instrument studies the same strip of ice at the same moment.

"Nadir-pointing imaging and spectral instruments, in combination with a ram-pointing dust detector and neutral gas mass spectrometer, observe the same real estate" [K25]; the attitude lets "all science instruments … operate and gather data simultaneously" [P24].

The radiation bill

Jupiter is wrapped in invisible radiation that slowly cooks electronics. Clipper's brain lives in a metal box called the vault, and the mission is planned so the total dose stays under a limit.

Tour design limit 3 Mrad total ionizing dose behind 100 mil of aluminium [P24]; the planned tour reaches 2.97 Mrad by the last Europa flyby [C25]. Vault walls: aluminium–zinc alloy up to 9.2 mm thick, bolted to the propulsion module [JPL]. Dose per flyby: unconfirmed.

The road to Europa

Launched 14 October 2024, Clipper borrowed speed from Mars in March 2025 and will borrow more from Earth in December 2026, arriving at Jupiter in April 2030.

Launch 14 Oct 2024, 12:06 pm EDT, LC-39A, expendable Falcon Heavy [NASA][C25]; arrays deployed the same day, magnetometer boom 5 Nov 2024 [Co25]; Mars gravity assist 1 Mar 2025 at 884 km [JPL]; Earth gravity assist Dec 2026 (pre-launch window 2–7 Dec; exact date and altitude unconfirmed) [JPL]; JOI 11 Apr 2030; end of prime mission 23 Jun 2034; Ganymede impact 3 Sep 2034 [P24][C25]. 2.9 billion km in 5½ years [JPL].

03 · Nadir deck

Four eyes on the ice

The bottom of the mug says four instruments "FACE +Y (NADIR)". They are the ship's eyes, and they all look straight down at Europa; three of them sit on a shelf on the +Y side called the nadir deck.

EIS is two cameras: a zoom lens that can see things half a metre across, and a wide lens that sees a strip 44 km wide. E-THEMIS sees heat, so it can spot warm cracks where the ocean might be close to the surface. UVS sees ultraviolet light and can sniff thin gas above the ice. MISE splits light into colours to tell salt from ice, and it bolts to the vault itself rather than the shelf.

Lower the altitude and watch the footprints shrink and sharpen. That is why Clipper dives so low.

"The optical remote sensing instruments (Europa-UVS, EIS NAC, EIS WAC, MISE, and E-THEMIS) have their apertures along the spacecraft's +Y direction … all except MISE are co-located on the 'nadir deck'"; MISE "is attached directly to the vault" [P24]. The deck is kinematically mounted to the vault for mechanical isolation [P24].

EIS [T24]: NAC 2.3° × 1.2° FOV, 10 µrad IFOV → 0.5 m/pixel over a 2 km swath from 50 km; 2-axis gimbal ±30° cross- and along-track. WAC 48° × 24° FOV, 218 µrad IFOV → 11 m/pixel at the centre of a 44 km swath from 50 km. Both 8-megapixel (4k × 2k). E-THEMIS [Ch24]: three bands, 7–14, 14–28 and 28–80 µm; sensor assembly on the nadir deck; built by Arizona State University. Europa-UVS [R24][P24]: 55–206 nm; airglow port, high-spatial-resolution port and a solar port 40° off the main boresight; FOV offset ∼0.6° ahead of Y toward +Z; built at SwRI. MISE [Bl24]: 0.8–5 µm at 10 nm resolution, 25 m/pixel at 100 km, 300 cross-track pixels (7.5 km swath at 100 km [D]), scan mirror.

Footprints here scale linearly with altitude from the 50 km figures; E-THEMIS and UVS widths are schematic (the fact sheet gives no FOV for them).

Why a deck, and why MISE is different

Cameras must point exactly the same way as each other, so they share one stiff shelf that is mounted so the rest of the ship cannot bend it. MISE is different: it bolts straight to the vault.

"the nadir deck, which is kinematically mounted to the vault for mechanical isolation" [P24]; MISE is attached directly to the vault [P24].

How sharp is half a metre?

From 50 km up, the zoom camera could tell a car from a van. Nobody has seen Europa that closely.

NAC 10 µrad IFOV: 0.5 m pixels at 50 km range over a 2 km swath [T24]; ground-track speeds up to ∼4.5 km/s drive the exposure design [T24].

04 · The +Z face

Catching gas and dust

The big yellow face on the mug is the front of the ship at closest approach, the side that hits whatever is floating above Europa. Two instruments sit on it with their mouths open.

MASPEX swallows gas and weighs each molecule, so it can tell water from salt from the stuff of life. SUDA catches dust grains that have been knocked off the surface; each grain hits so hard it turns into a tiny puff of charged atoms, and SUDA reads what it was made of.

Because they face forward and the cameras face down, the gas and dust they catch comes from the very ground the cameras are photographing.

"MASPEX and SUDA are oriented along the spacecraft's +Z axis, which will be oriented in the ram direction (the direction of spacecraft motion) during flybys" [P24]. The print places both on the +Z face at the −X edge; their exact X positions are as the mug shows.

MASPEX [W24][FK][NASA]: a multi-bounce time-of-flight mass spectrometer (MBTOF), 1–500 m/z, resolving power above 25,000 (FWHM); the FK frame's +Z is "along the axis of the drift tube, pointing towards the inlet … nominally co-aligned with the spacecraft +Z axis"; built by SwRI. SUDA [K25][D]: a time-of-flight, reflectron-type impact mass spectrometer; requirement "boresight ≤ 2.5° from Kepler Ram at C/A for at least 90 % of the planned flybys"; impacts at typical 4–5 km/s; the FK SUDA frame is rotated about +Z only, so its boresight is +Z.

The particle stream here is illustrative; no density or plume model is claimed.

Go deeper

Why speed is the detector

A dust grain hitting at four kilometres a second does not bounce; it vaporises into a flash of charged atoms. SUDA times those atoms as they drift to a sensor: heavy ones arrive late.

Impact ionisation at 4–5 km/s feeds a reflectron TOF analyser [K25]. MASPEX folds a long flight path into a multi-bounce tube to reach >25,000 resolving power [W24].

PIMS Upper, at the top of the yellow face

Above the yellow face on the mug is PIMS UPPER, a plasma catcher that also has a forward-facing cup. It gets its own station (07).

PIMS Upper carries RAM (+Z) and ANTI-NADIR (−Y) Faraday cups [FK]; see station 07.

05 · The wings

Wings that listen

The two purple lines on the mug are the solar wings, +X and −X. Spread out, they are as wide as a basketball court is long. At Jupiter the Sun is so faint that all that area makes only about 700 watts, what a coffee maker uses.

Along the edge of each wing hang the antennas of REASON, a radar that can see through ice. The wings turn so those edges point at Europa, then the radar sends pings down into the ice and listens for echoes from the bottom of the ice shell, maybe from the ocean itself.

Turn the wings, pick a frequency, and change the pretend ice thickness to see how the echo moves. The real thickness is one of the things Clipper is going to find out.

Each array is five panels, about 14.2 m × 4.1 m; together 102 m², span 30.5 m [JPL]; 700 W at end of mission [P24][NASA]; built by APL and Airbus (Leiden) [NASA]. Batteries: three lithium-ion, 365 Ah at end of mission [P24]. The arrays are single-axis articulated (CK frames EUROPAM_SA+X, SA−X); at zero position the cells face −Y [FK]. "PV SIDE" on the mug marks that cell face.

REASON [B24][NASA]: dual-frequency ice-penetrating radar, 9 and 60 MHz. Antenna subsystem mounted on the solar arrays: two 17.6 m HF dipole assemblies and four 2.76 m VHF folded dipoles, designated +X (starboard) / −X (port) and inner / outer for VHF; the HF antennas unfold perpendicular to the arrays. In SCA "the solar arrays at −90°, which puts REASON antenna facing nadir" [C25]; the cell side then faces ±Z, sign unconfirmed. The 3D model keeps the cells toward +Z.

The cutaway and echo timing are a what-if (two-way time ≈ 2d·n/c with n ≈ 1.78 for ice, general physics, not a REASON result). REASON's first full test was during the Mars flyby of 1 Mar 2025 [JPL PIA26568].

Go deeper

Why two frequencies

Low notes travel through ice further but see blurrier; high notes see finer detail near the top. Using both gives depth and detail.

Dual-frequency ice-penetrating radar, 9 and 60 MHz [B24]. The band roles are general radar practice, not quoted in the fact sheet: the lower frequency (HF) penetrates deeper, the higher (VHF) resolves finer; the mug's "REASON" bars sit at the +Y foot of each array line, matching the SCA geometry [C25].

Why the wings must turn

The antennas are glued to the wings, so to point the radar down you have to turn the whole wing. The cells then face sideways for a few hours around closest approach.

One-DOF array articulation [FK]; SCA fixes the arrays at −90° so the antenna edge points nadir [C25]. (The batteries, 365 Ah EOM [P24], are sized for such periods: our inference.)

06 · The boom

Feeling the ocean

The red arrow at the handle end of the mug points along a long pole, the magnetometer boom, which sticks out of the dish side of the ship. Along its outer part are three tiny compasses that feel magnetic fields.

Jupiter has a huge magnetic field, and as the planet spins the field washes over Europa, changing direction every few hours. If there is salty water under the ice, that changing field makes electric currents in the water, and those currents make a field of their own. The compasses can feel that answer. No ocean, no answer.

The pole keeps the compasses far from the ship's own electronics, which make small magnetic fields of their own.

ECM (Europa Clipper Magnetometer, formerly ICEMAG) [FK]: "three triaxial fluxgate magnetometers, FG1, FG2, and FG3, mounted on a 8.5-m long extendable boom at distances of 8.5-m, 6.8-m, and 5.2-m" [Co25]; deployed 5 November 2024 [Co25]. The boom is on the propulsion module [P24].

Boom axis ≈ (0, −0.94, −0.34) in the body frame: along −Y, tilted 20° toward −Z [D, FK ECM mount/mast matrices], consistent with the mug's arrow pointing to −Y. In the Sun-pointed cruise attitude "the boom points directly at the Sun" [Co25].

Science goal: "confirm that Europa's ocean exists, measure its depth and salinity, and measure the moon's ice shell thickness" [NASA]; the boom reduces "obstructions and magnetic interference from the spacecraft" [NASA]. The induction animation is a cartoon of the method (a conducting shell responding to a time-varying primary field), not a model of Europa's response; Jupiter's rotation period and field geometry are general background, not from the fact sheet.

Go deeper

Three compasses, not one

With three sensors at different distances the team can tell which part of the signal comes from the ship (it fades with distance) and which comes from space (it does not).

Three sensors at 8.5, 6.8 and 5.2 m [Co25]; using the fall-off with distance to separate the spacecraft's own field is standard magnetometer-boom practice (general).

PIMS helps the magnetometer

Charged gas around Europa also bends magnetic fields. PIMS measures that gas so the team can subtract its effect and keep only the ocean's answer.

"PIMS Faraday cups will distinguish those distortions from Europa's induced magnetic field" [NASA]; see station 07.

07 · PIMS

Four cups for plasma

Space near Jupiter is not empty. It is full of plasma, gas so hot that its atoms have lost electrons and carry electric charge. Plasma bends magnetic fields, so to read the ocean's magnetic answer (station 06) you must also measure the plasma.

PIMS does that with four Faraday cups: metal cups that catch charged particles and measure the tiny electric current they make. Two cups sit on top of the ship (PIMS UPPER on the mug), facing forward and up. Two sit low on the fuel module, under the handle (PIMS LOWER IS BELOW HANDLE), facing down and backward.

Drag the plasma direction around. Between them, the four cups look forward, back, down and up.

"PIMS consists of two separated sensors (PIMS Upper and PIMS Lower), each with two orthogonally oriented Faraday cups of 90° FOV" [P24], four cups in all [Co23]. FK cup frames: PIMS_UPPER → PIMS_RAM and PIMS_ANTI_NADIR; PIMS_LOWER → PIMS_ANTI_RAM and PIMS_NADIR [FK].

So Upper looks +Z and −Y, Lower looks −Z and +Y. PIMS Lower is on the propulsion module [P24], which matches the mug: "PIMS LOWER IS BELOW HANDLE" (the handle is −Z) and the lower sensor sits toward +Y. "The positions of PIMS Upper and PIMS Lower permit combined view of plasma around the spacecraft with only minor obstructions" [P24].

Purpose: "PIMS Faraday cups will distinguish those distortions from Europa's induced magnetic field" [NASA]. The cup positions on the body (other than the module and the faces) are as the mug shows.

Go deeper

What a Faraday cup measures

Charged particles fly into a metal cup and give it their charge. A meter counts how much charge arrives each second. That is a current, and it tells you how dense and how fast the plasma is.

A collector behind a grid set measures ion/electron current versus retarding potential, giving density, velocity and temperature over a 90° FOV per cup [P24].

Why "upper" and "lower"

Our best reading: on the launch pad the ship stands on its −Z end, so "upper" probably means nearer the top (+Z) and "lower" nearer the bottom (−Z). The mug's handle side is −Z, so lower is below the handle.

Z is the launch-vehicle centreline [FK]; the Avionics Module stacks on the Propulsion Module [S25]; PIMS Lower is a propulsion-module item [P24].

08 · The antennas

Talking to Earth

The mug's open top is −Y, and that is where the big dish lives: a 3-metre high-gain antenna that sends pictures home. To use it the ship must turn the dish at Earth, which it does between flybys.

During a flyby the dish faces away from Earth, because the cameras need +Y on Europa. So the ship talks through smaller antennas instead: three fan-beam antennas (FBA1, 2, 3) and three low-gain antennas (LGA1, 2, 3) that look in different directions. They cannot carry pictures, but they can carry a steady tone.

That tone is science too. As Europa's gravity tugs the ship, the tone's pitch wobbles, and from the wobble the team measures the moon's gravity. The mug calls it G&RS: gravity and radio science, done with the LGAs and FBAs.

"The Europa Clipper spacecraft is designed with a fixed X/Ka-band High Gain Antenna (HGA) and X-band Medium Gain Antenna (MGA) pointed in the spacecraft −Y direction … typical tracking with the HGA will not be possible during Europa flybys" [M23]. HGA: 3 m, X-band uplink, X and Ka downlink, up to 16 kb/s (X) and 500 kb/s (Ka) [P24][NASA]. Calibrated X and Ka boresights are within hundredths of a degree of −Y [FK].

Three X-band LGAs with symmetric beams are mounted in +Y, −Y and −Z [M23]; they give all-sky coverage but are of limited use beyond the near-Earth environment [P24]. Three X-band fan-beam antennas allow carrier-only Doppler tracking during Europa encounters; they lie in the Y/Z plane with the narrow beam axis along X [M23]. Official FBA names: FBA−Y+Z (FBA1), FBA−Y−Z (FBA2), FBA+Y−Z (FBA3) [FK]. LGA1/2/3 numbering is unconfirmed; the mug's groupings fit LGA1 = +Y, LGA2 = −Y, LGA3 = −Z (boresight 15° off −Z toward −Y [D]).

G&RS: the primary measurement is the tidal Love number k₂, "an independent diagnostic of the presence of a global subsurface ocean" [M23]; two-way X-band Doppler from DSN 70 m stations in a ±2 h window around C/A [M23]; usable antennas: 3 FBAs, 2 LGAs and the MGA [P24]. So the mug's "G&RS=LGAs+ FBAs+" holds, with the MGA as the "+". Seven smaller antennas in all [JPL][S25].

Go deeper

Why the dish doubles as a sunshade

Near Earth the Sun is fierce, so the ship turns the dish toward the Sun and hides behind it like a parasol.

"The highly isolated High Gain Antenna acts as a spacecraft sun shield during inner cruise including the Earth flyby" [S25]; Sun-pointing the HGA is "not the spacecraft's normal orientation (only for inner cruise to manage heat)" [Co25], so −Y points at the Sun then [D].

Fan beams

A fan beam is wide one way and thin the other, like light through a letterbox. Three of them, tilted differently, cover the directions Earth can be in during a flyby without any moving parts.

FBAs in the Y/Z plane, narrow axis along X; boresights (0, −0.71, +0.71), (0, −0.71, −0.71), (0, +0.71, −0.71) [D] [M23][FK].

Earth's turn, December 2026

In December 2026 Clipper swings past Earth to pick up speed, a few thousand kilometres up.

Pre-launch window 2–7 Dec 2026 at about 3,100–3,450 km (1,951–2,144 mi) depending on launch date [JPL]; the final date and altitude are unconfirmed by a NASA source.

09 · Wheels & thrusters

Turning the ship

The pale discs on the mug, split by the handle, hold four purple ovals: reaction wheels. Spin a heavy wheel one way and the ship turns the other way, with no fuel at all. Four wheels in different tilts can turn the ship any way it likes; any three are enough.

The four purple corners marked REM1 to REM4 are rocket engine modules, six small engines each. Sixteen point out of the −Z end to push the ship along; eight point up and down to roll it. Each engine pushes with about the weight of a 3-litre bottle of water, and up to eight fire together.

Drag the pad to turn with the wheels. Hold the buttons to fire the engines.

RWA: Honeywell HR18-250 reaction wheel assemblies on D-Strut isolators, "rotational flywheels mounted directly on the spacecraft in different orientations. The Europa Clipper spacecraft has four reaction wheels, requiring any set of three for nominal operations" [S25]; "two-foot-wide steel, aluminum, and titanium wheels" [NASA]; on the propulsion module [P24]. Positions and numbering RWA1–4: unconfirmed, drawn as the mug shows (one disc centred on −Z).

REM: "four of these Rocket Engine Modules, each with six individual rocket engines. Each engine is capable of delivering 27.5 N of thrust" [S25]. "Sixteen of these engines are pointed in the −Z direction, in two sets of eight for redundancy … eight more engines (two redundant sets of four) that point in the +Y and −Y directions … configured in coupled pairs and are used for roll control" [P24]. Up to eight at a time for 220 N [JPL]. Bipropellant: monomethylhydrazine with mixed oxides of nitrogen (3 %) [P24]. Propellant load about 2,750 kg of a 5,800 kg launch mass [JPL]. REM positions and numbering: unconfirmed, drawn as the mug shows.

The wheel animation conserves angular momentum schematically (body rate ∝ −wheel rate); the "unload" button illustrates momentum unloading with thrusters in general, not a documented Clipper procedure.

Go deeper

Why wheels fill up

Every time outside pushes (sunlight, gravity) nudge the ship, the wheels spin a bit faster to cancel it. They cannot spin faster forever, so now and then the engines give a tiny push while the wheels slow down.

Reaction wheels store angular momentum; external torques accumulate it until a thruster "desaturation" removes it. General spacecraft practice; Clipper's specific procedure is not in the fact sheet.

Which REM is which

No public document says which corner is REM1. The numbers here are copied from the mug.

Count (4 × 6 = 24 engines) and directions are sourced [S25][P24]; the mug places REM4 (+X, −Y), REM1 (+X, +Y), REM3 (−X, −Y), REM2 (−X, +Y), all on the −Z (handle) side [print].

10 · Sensors

Knowing which way you point

Before you can turn a ship you must know which way it is facing. The two blue barrels at the bottom of the mug, labelled SRUs, are star trackers: small cameras that photograph the stars and match them to a map, many times a second.

The mug also says DSS ON +Y. A digital Sun sensor is a simpler helper that just reports where the Sun is. There is one on the +Y side and one on the −Y side, so the ship can find the Sun from either side.

Press "Lose the stars" to see what happens when the moon blocks a tracker's view: the second one takes over.

SRU: "Europa Clipper's two stellar reference units, informally called 'star trackers'" [NASA], a modified Sodern HYDRA [S25]; first star images 4 Dec 2024 [NASA]. FK frames ST1/ST2 in a "56/52 configuration", ST2 "rotated 199 deg about the ST2 boresight" to match the as-installed state [FK]. Boresights ≈ (+0.51, −0.34, +0.79) and (−0.51, −0.34, +0.79): mostly +Z, 38° off +Z, 20° toward −Y, split ±X [D]. Mounting location: unconfirmed; drawn where the mug puts them (the +Y end under the +Z face).

DSS: "Digital sun sensors (procured from Adcole …) detect the position of the Sun relative to the spacecraft" [S25]. Two heads, EUROPAM_DSS+Y and DSS−Y (after "ECR-9502 DSS Head Relocation") [FK]; boresights DSS+Y ≈ (0, 0.99, −0.15), DSS−Y ≈ (0, −0.91, +0.42) [D]. The mug lists DSS both on +Y and in the −Y group with the MGA, matching the two heads.

The "lose the stars" sequence is illustrative; the fact sheet contains no attitude-estimation details.

Go deeper

Why star trackers look forward, not down

Probably because Europa and Jupiter are big and bright, and a camera full of moon is useless for finding stars. The trackers point mostly along +Z, away from the moon below, and slightly toward the dish side.

Boresights have a +0.79 Z component and −0.34 Y (anti-nadir) [D] [FK]; the SRU field of view is not in the fact sheet, so why they point this way is our inference.

Two of everything

Two trackers, two Sun sensors, four wheels when three would do, sixteen engines in two sets of eight. Spacecraft carry spares because nobody can fix them.

Redundancy patterns cited: 2 SRUs [NASA], 2 DSS heads [FK], 4 RWAs with any 3 sufficient [S25], −Z engines in two sets of eight [P24].

11 · The flight rule

"DO NOT ROTATE MINUS-Y TO EARTH UNLESS EMPTY."

Real missions have flight rules: written safety rules that every command is checked against before it is sent. The mug carries one of its own.

On the real ship, pointing −Y at Earth is the most normal thing it does: that is how the big dish sends data home. On a mug, −Y is the open top, and pointing it at Earth (down!) pours your coffee out. So the rule only applies "unless empty". Try it both ways.

The rule number, 02026DD, is probably a wink at a small asteroid called 2026 DD that was spotted in February 2026. That is Kevin's guess, not a fact.

No public Europa Clipper flight-rule document was found, and "FR 02026DD" has no public match. Flight rules in JPL practice are "expressed in English prose, and the operations team enforces them daily either by manual review or via automated checkers"; "All commands sent to Mars are either simulated or checked by hand against a database of Flight Rules" [Maimone et al. 2018, RP-check]. Pathfinder's rover rules were numbered by category with a rationale, affected commands, criticality and enforcement [PDS].

The joke inverts a real fact: for the spacecraft, −Y (HGA and MGA) toward Earth is the routine Earth-point attitude for downlink [M23], and −Y toward the Sun is the inner-cruise sunshade attitude [S25][Co25]. For the mug, −Y is the opening.

"02026DD": JPL's Small-Body Database lists (2026 DD), an Apollo-class near-Earth asteroid, not a PHA, a = 2.29 au, e = 0.653, i = 25.4°, observed 2026-02-14 to 2026-02-27 [SBDB]. That the rule number refers to it is Kevin's reading and unconfirmed. "02026" could equally wink at the December 2026 Earth flyby; also unconfirmed.

What a flight rule looks like

Rules like "never point the camera at the Sun" or "never fire the engines while the solar wings are turning" (made-up examples). Short, written down, checked before every command goes up.

Each rule carries a rationale, the commands it affects, a criticality and an enforcement method [PDS Pathfinder rules]; sequences are checked against the rule database before uplink [RP-check].

Why "unless empty" is also real-ish

The real ship has a condition of its own: during a flyby the dish cannot face Earth, because +Y has to stay on the moon; it turns back to Earth about half a day after closest approach.

Sun tracking and the turn to Earth-point resume about half a day after C/A [C25]; during the encounter the HGA points away from Earth [JPL].
